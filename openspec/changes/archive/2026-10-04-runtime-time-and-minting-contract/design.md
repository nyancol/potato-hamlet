# Design

## Context

See `proposal.md` (Why) for motivation and the spec deltas for the requirements. Four facts about the current artefacts shape the approach.

**The two shipped laws disagree about where time comes from.** `requisitions` reads the position from `clockOf g`, which resolves `ph:Clock`'s `ph:dayIndex` and `ph:atHour`, and its `Snapshot` carries `day` and `hour` as fields. `mayor-quorum` has no such function: its `Snapshot` is `{citizens, cycles, mayorId}` with no time at all, and `cycleOf g` reads `ph:dayIndex` and `ph:atHour` off each `MayorElectionCycle` individual. So the two laws derive their guards from different individuals, and the client's `set_time` writes both plus every process instance, with a docstring that concedes the clock "is what the framework fixes" but is insufficient for a law with the history shape.

**The oracle is already a second writer to the clock.** `mayor-quorum`'s `writeBack` opens with `g.setOne iriClock (ph "atHour") (.lit (toString (cur s).hour) ...)`, taking the hour from the current process instance. It writes `atHour` and not `dayIndex`, so after a `repeat` — where `cur s` is the freshly minted successor whose hour is `0` — the returned graph carries the day the consumer supplied and an hour of zero. `requisitions` does the same echo deliberately, both fields, with a comment saying why.

**Minting is a materialisation, not an event.** `writeBack` folds over `withIndex s.cycles` and calls `ensureCycle`, `ensureMintedCalendar`, and `ensureOccurrences` for every element, each guarded by "is this IRI already typed, if so skip", with single-valued properties going through `setOne`. The returned graph is the incoming graph extended by that encoding; it is not a delta and not a projection of the snapshot alone, since it takes the incoming graph as an argument and carries forward triples no law field holds — the four `ph:State` individuals, the hamlet, every citizen's `ph:age`.

**A gap in the calendar and a collision in an IRI both shipped.** `mayor-quorum/population.ttl` carries a `ph:CalendarSpan` with `ph:gapDays 30`, no `ph:dayIndex` and no `ph:atDate`, while the calendar requirement says every span co-locates a readable date with a day index. `requisitions`'s `reqIri` docstring records that keying the IRI on the citizen alone made a second lodgement overwrite a withdrawn first one, so the graph gained a start bound on a finished instance and the withdrawal was never recorded.

## Goals / Non-Goals

**Goals:**
- One individual in the graph carries the present, so every law responds to the same write and no consumer needs to know a law's internal shape.
- The properties the graph asserts become checkable, so "one writer", "no accumulation", "no collision", and "nothing lives only in Lean" are gate failures rather than conventions.
- Make `apply` a pure function of `(graph, act)` a stated property, which is what session persistence needs and what `law-simulation-tui`'s open question is waiting on.
- Keep the wire protocol untouched: no verb, no field, no new verdict key.

**Non-Goals:**
- Any change to how a law's rules are written, beyond where the current position is read from. The guards, the transition functions, and the witnesses stay as they are.
- `ph:gapDays` anchoring. The relative form is admitted; what a gap resolves against is not decided here (see Open Questions).
- A `tick` verb, or moving the clock behind the oracle. The clock stays the runtime's write.
- Making the TUI's diff of two graphs a framework concern. It stays a presentation detail in the consumer.

## Decisions

**1. `ph:Clock` is the only carrier of the present; the per-instance cell goes.** `mayor-quorum`'s `Snapshot` gains `day` and `hour` read from the clock, matching `requisitions`, and `cycleOf` stops reading them off the cycle. Every guard that currently reads `(cur s).day` reads `s.day`. This is a mechanical substitution with one trap: `cur s`'s hour is also used for poll hours, so `voteDue?` and `pollsClosed?` must read the clock's hour rather than the cycle's. `Cycle.day` and `Cycle.hour` survive only as the schedule seed `freshCycle` writes for a newly minted instance — and that seed is what the `ph:dayIndex` domain then forbids, so it is dropped too, since a successor's schedule already comes from `electionDayK (k+1)`. *Alternative considered:* keeping the per-instance cell and naming it. Rejected: it needs a class, a meaning, and a writer, and "how many places does this law keep the day" is exactly the question the client is already answering by guesswork.

**2. The domain is declared, so the loophole is closed rather than documented.** `ph:dayIndex` gains the `rdfs:domain gfo-light:TimeBoundary` that `ph:atHour` already carries, and the gate rejects any subject outside that category carrying either property. This is why the fix is cheap: the ontology already says where an hour may sit, and the day index was simply missing from the same sentence. It also catches a second, separate redundancy — `ensureMintedCalendar` writes `ph:dayIndex` on the nomination-window span, whose start is already named by its `temporallyStartedBy` bound pointing at a minted point that carries the same day. *Alternative considered:* leave the domain off and rely on review. Rejected: an undeclared domain is invisible to the gate, so the first law to reintroduce a per-instance position would pass review and fail nothing.

**3. Stability is enforced by declaring cardinality, not by testing for churn.** The ontology declares `owl:FunctionalProperty` on every single-valued property, and the gate checks both that the declaration is present and that no subject holds two values after an accepted act. The declaration alone is what a consumer needs in order to read the graph correctly; the behavioural check is what stops a `setOne` regressing to an append, which is the failure `rules.lean:1183` already documents once. *Alternative considered:* testing churn by replaying acts and diffing graphs. Rejected as the primary check because the diff is exactly what the declaration makes unnecessary, and because a behavioural test cannot tell a first write from a second one.

**4. Freshness is stated as a rule about keying, not about counting.** The requirement says an IRI is a function of the dimensions along which the fact can repeat, and explicitly that it is not a recipe for an unused name and does not count existing individuals. That is what makes re-encoding a no-op without any state about what has been minted, and it is what the wire format's unorderedness forces: a counter would make a name depend on the order acts arrived in. *Alternative considered:* requiring a global mint counter per class. Rejected: it reintroduces order-dependence into a format that has none, and it makes `apply` non-deterministic under permutation of the input.

**5. The collision check goes in the gate, and the graph alone cannot catch it.** Every other check in the gate passes on a collided graph: both individuals are typed by declared classes, no subject was removed, and the verdict shape is unchanged. So the gate compares each newly materialised individual's IRI against what is already there and fails when the encodings differ. *Alternative considered:* have `apply` return a rejection. Rejected: a collision is a defect in the law's keying, which the review gate exists to catch before the artefact set is frozen, not a runtime condition a driver should have to interpret.

**6. A seed describes what the law permits, never how the moment is worked out — and no anchor is declared for a gap.** The calendar requirement now distinguishes a node fixed at review, which co-locates `ph:atDate` with `ph:dayIndex`, from a span whose end is a fixed interval after a moment that does not yet exist, which carries `ph:gapDays` and no absolute position. The shipped `repeat-gap` span becomes conforming rather than the requirement being bent to fit it.

What the requirement does *not* do is ask the span to declare what its interval is measured from, and this is the substantive part of the decision. A gap is the law leaving a moment to the executor, so the executor's method is the oracle's business; a declared anchor would put the computation back into the artefacts, which is the mistake `ph:gapDays` exists to avoid. `mayor-quorum` gets this right in its Lean and wrong in its seed, which is what surfaced the question: `rules.lean` computes the successor as `baseElectionDay + 30·k` while `population.ttl:137` asserts the gap "is measured from the cycle's own runtime-minted finish bound". Those are different methods for the same interval, and the seed's claim is not one the oracle honours. Left alone, the ontology describes a measurement the oracle does not perform.

So the change adds the inverse of what I first proposed: not "declare the anchor" but "the seed SHALL NOT describe one". A seed individual SHALL NOT carry a value whose only purpose is to let a consumer reproduce a computation the oracle owns. That covers both the anchor claim and, more generally, a seed value that no condition reads. `ph:repeatGapDays 30` is exactly that and is deleted — the interval the law fixes belongs in `ph:gapDays` on the span that bounds the phase, and the oracle's `repeatGap` is the only other place it needs to exist. *Alternative considered:* require the anchor, so the ontology cannot describe a measurement the oracle does not perform. Rejected: it makes the seed a second source for the computation rather than removing one. The cost of this choice is that a repeat performed late and a repeat performed on time are indistinguishable in the artefacts, which is correct — the artefacts describe what the law permits, and the graph records what happened.

**7. `mints:` is reworded from a promise to an affordance.** Under materialisation, which individuals exist follows from the state, so an ECA cannot fail to create a Process instance without the state being wrong. The declaration therefore says which class to look for after the act, and the requirement says so explicitly. This is the one requirement the change makes weaker. *Alternative considered:* dropping `mints:` and having consumers diff. Rejected: the diff is consumer-local and session-scoped, whereas the declaration is in the frozen manifest and available before anything runs, which is what makes it usable as a display hint.

**8. The oracle echoes the clock; it does not write it.** `requisitions`' echo with its comment is kept and generalised to both fields. The reason is not tidiness but the second writer: with the clock the graph's only free variable, an act that overwrote it would make the graph a function of something other than `(graph, act)`, and the hour-zeroing on `repeat` is exactly that. The gate checks it by sending a graph with a known clock position and asserting the returned `ph:Clock` carries it unchanged.

**9. A new meaning is a Role, and the acting-on-what is `playedBy`.** This is the substantive addition, and it uses vocabulary the framework already declares. `ph:relates` and `ph:qualityOf` are grounded in GFO and no `rules.lean` writes either, so the concept is not missing — it is unclaimed. The decision is to give it a place.

A transformation is expressed as a `gfo-light:Role` individual: the sheet plays `ph:BallotVote` in `ph:MayorElectionCycle r0`, and `ph:roleIn` names the cycle while `ph:playedBy` names the sheet. The quality acquired — the candidate's name — attaches to the sheet through `ph:qualityOf`. So the act's effect is entirely relational:

```
  BEFORE prepare                       AFTER prepare
  ------------------------------------------------------------------
  sheet/r0/alice  a ph:Sheet           sheet/r0/alice  a ph:Sheet
  pen/r0/alice    a ph:Pen               ph:playedBy  -> sheet/r0/alice
  (a ph:BallotVote role                 ph:qualityOf -> "Bob"
   would not exist)                    ballot/r0/alice  a ph:BallotVote
                                        ph:roleIn    -> election/r0
                                        ph:playedBy  -> sheet/r0/alice
```

Three properties of this shape are why it is a Role rather than a type on the individual. The same sheet can hold a different role in each process it takes part in, because `roleIn` differs — one sheet, a ballot in r0 and something else in r1. The individual never changes its class, so a consumer asking what the sheets are gets a stable answer while the process unfolds rather than one that depends on progress. And the role individual is what the freshness rule already governs, since its IRI is a function of the holder and the context, which is exactly the pair that must never collide.

Placing it in the existing Role machinery rather than a new `ph:BallotVote` relation also means the framework's existing rule — `ph:roleIn` names the context, `ph:playedBy` the holder — does the work unchanged, and a law needing a further meaning in a further process adds a Role class rather than a new relation. *Alternative considered:* let the individual's type change, so a `ph:Sheet` becomes a `ph:BallotVote` mid-process. Rejected: it makes "which individuals are the ballots" a function of how far the process has got, and it contradicts the framework's existing rule that an instance is read through its bounds and flags. *Alternative considered:* a new `ph:transforms` relation between the before and after individuals. Rejected: it needs two individuals for one thing, and the pair is not independent — it is the same sheet.

**10. Act inputs are declared and must be present; out-of-thin-air creation is refused.** A transformation declares the individuals it acts on, and the oracle refuses the act when any of them is absent from the graph, naming it. This is what makes the concept bite: `applyPrepare` currently names no inputs and creates forty individuals, so it would be refused until the law declares the sheets and pens it acts on. The requirement is deliberately narrow — it constrains what an act may bring into existence, not what it may change about an individual — because a law may still mint a process instance, a calendar node, or a bound, none of which is a physical thing and all of which are legitimate. *Alternative considered:* require every individual in the graph to be traceable to a seed or to an act. Rejected: it is the same rule stated as a graph invariant, and it cannot say which individuals a given act is responsible for, so a refusal would name no cause.

**11. Transformation semantics land before the shared vocabulary, not with it.** The two are separable and the order matters. Transformation semantics say what an act may do to an individual that exists; the shared physical ontology says which individuals exist for every law. A law can adopt the first while still declaring its own paper class, and the shared vocabulary can then be added without either depending on the other's shape. Stating them together would make the shared vocabulary look like a prerequisite for transformation semantics, which it is not. *Alternative considered:* one change covering the vocabulary, the transformation semantics, and the `mayor-quorum` rewrite. Rejected: it would put a change to what every law must seed in the same unit as a change to what an act may do, and `law-simulation-tui` would wait on both.

**12. "Replacing it wholesale rather than merging into it" is reworded, not removed.** The sentence is correct about consumer discipline and misleading about the oracle, whose returned graph *is* the incoming graph extended. The new wording states the oracle's semantics first and then the consumer's obligation, because a reader who takes the old sentence as describing the oracle would implement a splice and lose every individual the law has created. The README's "Never merge" gets the same treatment.

**13. The runtime owns the world's inputs; the oracle owns only its interpretation.** This is the principle that settles the clock, and it reaches further than the clock. The runtime advances time and may bring a physical thing into being. The oracle decides what the state means — which duties are due, which Role an individual holds, which individual a verdict names — and may mint a *meaning*, an occurrence, a process instance, a calendar node or a bound, none of which is a thing a duty is performed on. Neither the oracle nor a law creates the world's furniture.

Applied to the clock, it retires the echo. Both laws were writing `ph:Clock` back from the snapshot on every `apply`, which is what made the echo a second writer; removing it resolves a conflict that had surfaced as a false positive in the gate. `clockOf` still floors at every recorded moment, so an act is never evaluated against a rewound clock — but the floor is a **read**, and the runtime's value is left exactly as found. That distinction is the whole content of the fix: *refuse to act on a rewound clock* is monotonicity; *rewrite the clock* would be the oracle editing a log with a single writer. The gate's `clock untouched by apply` is now a true check rather than one that had to be weakened.

The principle also settles what `check_transformation_inputs` is for. It reports that `applyPrepare` conjures nine `ph:BallotVote` individuals, and the report is right — but the fix is not to let the oracle mint ballots more carefully. It is that a ballot is a *meaning* a sheet takes on, so the honest rewrite gives each citizen a sheet (which the runtime seeds, per `shared-physical-vocabulary`) and has the act give it a Role. That is `mayor-quorum-transformation-rewrite`, and this principle is why it is the right shape rather than merely the available one.

### Known finding, accepted at this change's close

`applyPrepare` creates nine `ph:BallotVote` individuals out of nothing, on both election flavours, and the gate reports it and keeps the sets unreviewable:

```
--- transformation inputs: FAIL ---
  transformation: `prepare` brought 9 new ph:BallotVote individual(s) into existence,
  and it is neither a Role the individual takes on nor an occurrence of a declared
  phase, so nothing accounts for it appearing.
```

Accepted rather than fixed here, and the reason is decision 13 rather than expedience: the oracle may not create a physical thing, so the fix is not to mint ballots more carefully. A ballot is a *meaning a sheet takes on*, which means the runtime seeds a sheet per citizen and the act confers a Role. That is `mayor-quorum-transformation-rewrite`, which already owns seeded sheets, `prepare` split into making and delivering, and ballots-as-Roles. Fixing it here would have meant inventing a vocabulary this change deliberately declined to invent.

Two artefact sets are therefore unreviewable at this change's close, which the gate is right to report. The follow-up is a dependency, not an optional extra.

### Follow-ups, in dependency order

- **`mayor-quorum-transformation-rewrite`** — seeds the physical world, splits `prepare` into making and delivering, makes the delivered flag state, makes a ballot a Role a sheet takes on. Closes the finding above and depends on `shared-physical-vocabulary` for the sheet, pen and mailbox classes.
- **`shared-physical-vocabulary`** — the shared objects, actions and capabilities, available to every law and required of none.
- A law may record the moment a period closed by declaring a property for it (decision 14); neither shipped election law does, and that is a lawful choice rather than a gap.

**14. No framework vocabulary for a period-closing moment, because a law already has one.** A `Chronoid` carries one start and one finish, so the moment an *intermediate* period within a process's life closed is not in the graph. `mayor-quorum` shows it: after `count` the graph carries `ph:counted` and no bound, so a reader cannot see that the polls closed at day 10 hour 19 — only that the cycle began at day 0 hour 10 and, later, finished. The oracle works around it with a flag, and `pollsOpen?` requires `!counted` precisely because the moment itself is unrecorded.

Three ways to close that were considered. **A `Chronoid` with more than one start/finish** is not a second use of `temporallyStartedBy`, it is a different relation wearing the name, and GFO's singular start and finish are what make a Chronoid readable as one interval. **A clock high-water mark** — an individual recording the furthest moment reached — is a *derived* value, the maximum over recorded bounds; making it an individual would put a copy of something the oracle owns into the graph, which is the error `ph:repeatGapDays` was and was deleted for. **Per-period bounds declared by each law** is the expressive option and needs no framework change either, but it hands every law a way to record a moment the framework has no opinion about, and no shipped law needs one: `requisitions` records the moments that matter to it — lodged, withdrawn, decided — as its own bounds, reading the clock for each.

So the decision is that the framework adds nothing, and says so. A law that needs the exact moment declares a property for it, which is already expressible and already checked: `ph:pollsClosedAt` grounded as a subproperty of `gfo-core:dateTimeValue` on `ph:MayorElectionCycle` passes `check_grounding` and `check_tbox` untouched, because it is an ordinary declared fact. The requirement's job is therefore to make the *limit* explicit — a missing record is a law's choice, not an oversight, and no consumer may reconstruct the moment from a flag or a calendar point. *Alternative considered:* mandate it, so every period-closing is recorded. Rejected: it is a requirement no shipped law could satisfy without new vocabulary, satisfied by none, and checkable by none — a law could declare the property and never write it.

## Risks / Trade-offs

- **[Risk] The monotonicity requirement has no natural enforcement point**, since the clock is written by the consumer and the oracle cannot refuse a value it is required to be total over. → Mitigation: enforce it where it is observable, which is on the *derived* facts — the gate sets the clock back after a period closed and asserts the period does not reopen and no act is admitted into the finished process. That is the behaviour a backwards clock would corrupt, and it is checkable without giving the oracle a new verb.
- **[Risk] The `ph:dayIndex` domain is a breaking change to all three shipped artefact sets at once**, and `requisitions` carries a second copy on each requisition individual that exists for a reason the substitution may not preserve. → Mitigation: the per-requisition stamp is only ever read as a fallback in `startDayOf`/`finishDayOf`, which `stampStartAt`/`stampFinishAt` already supply from the clock, so removing it should be a deletion rather than a rewiring; task ordering takes the oracle change before the ontology declaration so the build stays green.
- **[Risk] Functional-property declarations are per-law and easy to under-declare**, and a law that under-declares one still passes review unless the behavioural half of the check is also present. → Mitigation: the gate carries both halves, and the behavioural half is what actually catches the regression; the declaration half is what lets a consumer read the graph without guessing.
- **[Trade-off] Totality of the graph is expensive to check**, since reproducing every verdict from the graph alone means a walk per artefact set. → Mitigation: fold it into the existing graph round-trip check rather than adding a second walk, reusing the declared-act walk the gate already performs for the erasure check.
- **[Trade-off] Declaring `mints:` weaker removes a guarantee a future author might have relied on.** Accepted: the guarantee was never real — under materialisation no declaration can make creation happen — and a law author who believed it would be misled in a way this rewording prevents.
- **[Risk] The transformation requirement is not met by any shipped law on the day it lands**, so the first thing it does is report `mayor-quorum` as non-compliant. → Mitigation: the input-declaration check reports rather than fails until the `mayor-quorum` rewrite lands, and the check itself is verified against a scratch law that does declare its inputs. Making the gate fail a frozen set the moment the requirement appears would block the rewrite behind this change.
- **[Risk] Roles already carry office-holding meaning, so a paper playing a Role may read oddly to a consumer** that expects every `ph:playedBy` to be a citizen. → Mitigation: `ph:roleIn` and `ph:playedBy` are declared as GFO `roleIn`/`playedBy`, which are not citizen-specific, and the requirement states that `playedBy` names whatever holds the role. The alternative — a separate relation for non-citizen holders — would double the vocabulary for one case.
- **[Risk] Seed parity is hard to check, because "no condition reads this property" is a statement about the oracle's code rather than about the graph.** → Mitigation: check it the only way it can be checked honestly — drive a walk, collect every property any guard reads, and fail when the seed carries a `ph:` property outside that set. A seed value nothing reads is then a review failure rather than an invisible second source.
- **[Risk] A law that genuinely needs a per-instance position would be blocked** by the domain declaration rather than accommodated. → Mitigation: none needed yet, and deliberately so. The four things a per-instance position is usually mistaken for — an instance's place in its own schedule, how far it has got, elapsed time, and a running count — are all already carried by the index, the bounds, or a separate integer, and adding vocabulary for a case that has not appeared would be the framework inventing a second present tense on no evidence.

## Migration Plan

No wire or protocol migration. `mayor-quorum` is the only law whose rules change; `requisitions` changes only by dropping the per-requisition stamp, and its `Snapshot` already reads the clock. Both ontologies gain the `ph:dayIndex` domain and the functional declarations, and `prompts/onto-v2.md` gains the domain and the declaration so a redraft produces them. All three artefact sets are re-frozen after the gate passes. Rollback is per-artifact: reverting the ontology declaration restores the permissive reading, and reverting the oracle restores the per-instance cell, at the cost of the framework's scenario being false for `mayor-quorum` again.

## Open Questions

The question this change began with — whether a relative span must declare what its gap is measured from — is settled, and not by choosing an anchor. A seed describes what the law permits and never how the moment is worked out; the oracle computes the moment when the act that needs it arrives, whether that is the instance's initialisation or some later trigger. So the gap needs no declared anchor, and the artefacts must not describe one. See decision 6 and the seed-parity requirement it adds.

That settles the anchor but leaves the surrounding question open, which is narrower and better:

- **Should a minted point that resolves a declared gap be legible as that resolution** in the graph — that is, carry something saying it came from the law's interval rather than from a fixed calendar? The appeal of a law leaving a moment to the executor is that the interpretation is visible; if the resolution is recorded only as a resolved point, the interpreter's reasoning is invisible and the vagueness has become a hidden oracle. Traceability rather than taxonomy would be enough, and a gap that resolves to zero or to another gap would then be a law that does not say what it means. Deferrable: the resolved points are already correct and displayable, so nothing breaks without it.
- **Should the schedule of a future minted instance be available before the act that mints it?** The quorum violation announces the repeat date only in `detail`, which the framework says not to parse, and forbids the consumer computing the date. So a guide cannot show "next election: 21 February" until the repeat has been performed. Either a structured reading becomes expected for a law that defers a moment, or that display is acknowledged as unavailable. Related: if a gap's resolution is legible, a preview of it may follow from the same mechanism.
- **Should a span that describes an interval rather than a phase have vocabulary of its own?** `scripts/test_concurrency.py` needed a span bracketing a process's last scheduled moment, and found one that already exists: a quorum repeat mints its own nomination window, bounded by its announce and election points, which contains polls close. So the test uses a real, phase-marked span and no new vocabulary was required. What remains undeclared is the general case — a span bounding the interval *between* two recurrences, such as a mayorial term from one election to the next, has no phase to mark because it is not a phase of any process. Today such a span is legal by omission (every *phase* must have a span; nothing requires a span to have a phase) and a law may declare one locally, but nothing names what such a span *is*. A `ph:CyclicalProcess` class for a law to mark its recurrences with would say so. Deferred: no shipped law needs it, and `shared-physical-vocabulary` is the natural home for new vocabulary rather than this change.
